Q3. Show your edited code for `duplicateNegatives` that fixes the problem with the infinite loop.
A3.
void duplicateNegatives(Queue<int>& q) {
    int initialSize = q.size();
    for (int i = 0; i < initialSize; i++) {
        int val = q.dequeue();
        q.enqueue(val);
        if (val < 0) {
            q.enqueue(val);   // double up on negative numbers
        }
    }
}

Q17. In a short paragraph, describe a real or plausible scenario in which reworking an algorithm to improve its efficiency might benefit Earth's environment or humanity as a whole. Include your thoughts on how a software engineer working on this piece of code might identify such potential benefits and take them into consideration when designing the code.
A17. One scenario where networking an algorithm to improve its efficiency would benefit Earth's environment is the scenario of AI data centers. Over the past few years, media has covered multiple stories about how destructive AI data centers can be for global warming. Data centers emit lots of heat and require water to cool down the servers which process each query. In the case of a software engineer working at an AI data center, they can implement smarter searching algorithms to reduce the overall compute required to run each query. These small fixes will compound over time and save time, money, compute, and water needed to run the center. This will overall benefit Earth's environment. 

Q18. According to the contract that IBM struck with the state of Indiana, the criteria for optimization were improving efficiency of the overall welfare system and reducing fraud. Criteria for reducing wait times and wrongful denials were not included.  However, wrongfully denying benefits has a huge negative impact on the citizens who rely on the system.  If criteria like minimizing wrongful denials were not included in the contract, should engineers have included them in their optimization algorithm?  Why or why not?
A18. I believe that the engineers should have implemented the new criterion because, despite the contract not requiring them to add these features, the new criterion help achieve the fundamental goal of connecting people with the benefits they are entitled to. The required optimization fixes, while they made the system "faster", shut people out of benefits arbitrarily and denied people if they had even a single page of documentation missing. This made the system less ethical and only helped the state, not the people. Since the software engineers had a strong understanding of how the system worked and its limitations, I believe it was their duty to implement changes to safeguard people from getting shut out from benefits they deserved. Even if it wasn't in their contract, the IBM engineers had an obligation to build a system that wasn't just efficient, but ethical as well. 

Q19. Imagine that you took CS106B several years ago, and after completing CS106B, you were hired at Twitter as an engineer working on the image cropping system. How might you have approached setting the goals of this system? Is there a way you could design the procedures for code testing and release in your group to avoid the severe negative impacts on users of the system that are outlined in the case study?
A19. If I was one of the Twitter engineers tasked with working on the image cropping system, I would have changed the "entropy" approach to better represent a wider group of people. While the "entropy" approach works in theory, by isolating people's faces from backgrounds, it is clear that the algorithm wasn't tested on a wide variety of test data. I would be sure to implement a wide dataset of images with people of all genders, races, and ages. I would also be sure to build a dev team which itself was diverse, because a diverse dev team can better understand the ethical limitations of a product. By testing the algorithm on a wider variety of test data, and building with a diverse team, I could better ensure that representational harms don't affect the users.
